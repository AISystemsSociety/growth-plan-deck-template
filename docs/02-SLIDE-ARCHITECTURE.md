# The 20 slides, and why each one is there

The order of these slides is the argument. Each slide only lands because the one before it did its
job. Before you move, merge or delete anything, read the reason it exists.

This structure comes from a deck that has been presented on live sales calls dozens of times. It
borrows its spine from Alex Hormozi's value equation and close structure, and from classic direct
response: agitate the problem, present the mechanism, prove it, price it, reverse the risk, close.

---

## The arc in one line

**Pain → Reframe → Mechanism → Proof → Price → Risk removal → Close.**

If you only remember one thing: you do not get to talk about price until the prospect has agreed on
the problem, believed the mechanism, and seen proof. Slides 1 to 9 buy you the right to slide 10.

---

## Block 1: Pain (slides 1 to 5)

You are not selling yet. You are getting agreement that a problem exists and that it is expensive.

### Slide 1 · COVER
Sets the frame before you say a word. The headline is the dream outcome stated as a specific
number, not an adjective. "Ten qualified meetings a month" beats "more leads".

The three stat cards below it are your three differentiators. They pre-answer "why you". Pick the
three things a competitor genuinely cannot say.

**Do not** put your logo at four times the size of theirs. This deck is about them.

### Slide 2 · THE PROBLEM
Twist the knife. Describe their current situation in their own words, ideally quoting the discovery
call directly. Symptoms, not diagnosis.

The register matters: describe the pain, never blame them for it. The moment a prospect feels
judged, they defend the status quo instead of examining it.

### Slide 3 · THE SOLUTION
The reframe. What is actually causing the symptoms on slide 2, and what a fix looks like. The six
benefit slots are deliberately about their life, not your features. Time back, control, predictable
revenue, not "automated workflows".

### Slide 4 · OLD WAY vs NEW WAY
Why the obvious approach fails. This slide exists to kill the three alternatives in their head
before they raise them: do nothing, hire someone, or use a cheaper competitor.

Three reasons each side. Not four, not two. Three reads as considered; more reads as defensive.

### Slide 5 · COST OF INACTION
The first number in the deck, and it is THEIR number, not your price. What one month of not solving
this costs them.

This slide is what makes your price look small later. If you skip it, slide 10 is the first number
they see, and every price is expensive in a vacuum.

The maths must be conservative and it must be theirs. Use their average deal value and their close
rate. Inflated numbers here destroy trust for the rest of the deck.

## Block 2: Mechanism and proof (slides 6 to 9, 13 to 15)

### Slide 6 · THE OFFER
The value equation. Why what you are offering is structured the way it is. This is the slide that
separates you from someone selling the same service without having thought about it.

### Slide 7 · MARKET SIZE
Is there actually enough opportunity here to be worth doing? This protects both of you. A prospect
whose market is too small should not buy, and telling them so buys enormous credibility.

Put a real screenshot of your market research here. A number you assert is a claim. A number they
can see you pulled is proof.

### Slide 8 · HOW IT WORKS
The mechanism, step by step. Five steps is the sweet spot. Fewer feels hand-wavy, more feels
complicated.

The purpose is not to teach them to do it themselves. It is to prove there IS a method, and that
you have run it before. Show the sequence, not the tutorial.

### Slide 9 · THE TEAM
Who actually does the work. Buyers do not buy systems, they buy the belief that a competent human
is accountable.

If you are solo, keep one card and say so. One honest card beats three padded ones. The cost
comparison at the bottom (what these people would cost to hire directly) is doing quiet work: it
anchors your price against salaries, which are always bigger.

### Slide 13 · THE ROI MATH
The money slide, and the most important one in the deck. Three scenarios at three close rates,
with **net to them** as the hero number.

Three scenarios, not one, because a single projection reads as a sales promise. Three reads as
honest modelling. Make the pessimistic one genuinely pessimistic.

The numbers must reconcile with slide 5. If your cost of inaction and your ROI maths use different
assumptions, a sharp prospect will catch it and you lose the room.

### Slide 14 · CASE STUDIES
Proof. Everyone can make the same promise. Nobody can have the same proof.

Specific beats polished. "Cut their cost per booked call from X to Y in six weeks" beats "great
results". Two real case studies beat three invented ones. Delete cards you cannot fill honestly.

### Slide 15 · DELIVERY CRITERIA
The definition slide. Exactly what counts as one delivered unit of your work.

This is the single most underrated slide in the deck. It prevents the argument that otherwise
happens in month two, and its precision signals that you have done this enough times to have been
burned. Keep it short and specific.

## Block 3: The offer and the close (slides 10 to 12, 16 to 20)

### Slide 10 · THE PRICING
Two options side by side, with the recommended one visually dominant. Two options changes the
question from "yes or no" to "which one", which is a much easier question.

Hard rule: both cards' descriptions must run to the same number of lines. Uneven cards look broken
and pull the eye to the wrong one.

### Slide 11 · WHAT YOU GET
Itemise everything. A price with one line under it is a number. A price with fifteen line items
under it is a bargain.

Total the list at the bottom, then show the price beneath it.

### Slide 12 · THE BONUSES
Three things stacked on top, each with a struck-through value. Bonuses work because they arrive
after the price is anchored, so they land as pure gain.

Make them real things you actually hand over. A fake bonus is detectable and it costs you the
credibility you spent nineteen slides building.

### Slide 16 · THE GUARANTEE
Risk reversal. The single highest-leverage slide for conversion.

Only promise what you actually control. A guarantee you cannot honour is worse than no guarantee,
because you will eventually have to break it in front of someone who trusted you. Guarantee your
own outputs, not their business outcomes.

### Slide 17 · IS THIS SAFE?
The objection you know is coming. Every industry has one specific fear. Name it out loud before
they do.

Raising an objection yourself is disarming. Waiting for them to raise it means you are defending
instead of leading.

### Slide 18 · THE CHOICE
Three ways to solve the problem: build it in-house, piece it together from vendors, or work with
you. Priced honestly.

The in-house column should be genuinely fair. If you strawman it, they stop trusting the other two
columns.

### Slide 19 · TIMELINE
What happens after they say yes, week by week. This slide converts abstract commitment into a
concrete sequence, which reduces the fear of the unknown.

It also sets expectations you will be held to. Be conservative here.

### Slide 20 · SUMMARY and FAQ
Four facts they should leave remembering, then the FAQ, then one call to action.

The FAQ is not filler. It handles the objections that surface after the call, when you are not in
the room to answer them. Cover price, timing, what if it does not work, how much of their time it
takes, and what happens when the engagement ends.

**One call to action.** Multiple calls to action split intent and nothing gets clicked.

## Changing the structure

You can safely:
- Delete a slide you cannot fill honestly. An empty slide is worse than a missing one.
- Rewrite every word.
- Reorder within a block.

Be careful:
- Moving pricing (slide 10) earlier. Every slide before it is building the value that makes the
  price reasonable. Move it forward and you are quoting into a vacuum.
- Deleting slide 5 or slide 15. They look skippable. They are the two that do the most quiet work.
- Adding slides. Twenty is already long for a live call. The discipline is subtraction.

If you delete a slide, renumber the rest so `data-slide` runs 1 to N with no gaps. Navigation reads
that attribute, not document order.
