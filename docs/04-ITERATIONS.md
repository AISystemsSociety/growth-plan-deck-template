# What changed, and why

This deck did not arrive looking like this. It got here through roughly fifty rounds of edits, almost all of them made straight after a real sales call, when something in the room had just gone wrong. A prospect stalled on a slide, asked a question the deck should have answered, or read a number the wrong way, and the deck changed that afternoon.

This file exists so you do not have to rediscover the same lessons one call at a time. Each entry says what the deck used to do, what it does now, and why it moved. The "why" is the part worth reading.

---

## Pricing presentation

**One price became two tiers.**
Before: a single monthly floor plus one performance fee per qualified meeting.
Now: two tiers side by side. Both run the same engine, the same qualification gate, the same team. The only difference is sending volume and the floor that pays for it. Both tiers share the exact same performance fee.
Why: a single price forces a yes or no. Two tiers turn the question from "do I want this" into "which one do I want", which is a much easier question for someone to say yes to. It also stopped losing smaller prospects who wanted in but not at full volume. The performance fee was deliberately held identical across both tiers, so the choice is about capacity, never about how much of a discount someone can argue for.

**The highlighted card moved from the floor to the performance fee.**
Before: the monthly floor card carried the "featured" ribbon.
Now: the performance card is featured, and the ribbon says something like "pay per meeting" rather than "monthly floor".
Why: whichever card you highlight is the one the prospect reads as the offer. Highlighting the fixed fee makes it look like a retainer. Highlighting the per-meeting fee puts the emphasis on the part where you carry the risk, which is the whole argument.

**The title slide stopped carrying a price.**
An early deck put the price on the cover. It got removed in one of the first rounds and never came back.
Why: leading with price invites the prospect to shop before any value has been built. Price should land after the problem, the market, and the mechanism, not before.

**"At cost" and "no markup" were deleted everywhere.**
Before: the infrastructure list was labelled as billed at literal supplier cost with no markup, and that phrasing appeared in the deck, the contract, and the invoice.
Now: the stack is still itemised line by line, but with no commentary about margin at all.
Why: two reasons. It handed the prospect a lever to negotiate the one number that has no room in it, and it invited a line by line audit of costs that move around month to month. The itemised list still does its job, which is proving the fee is real work and real tooling. Saying "we make nothing on this" out loud made the deck defensive. The pricing now speaks for itself.

**A cost breakdown is not a value stack.**
An itemised list of real costs adding up to the real fee reads as honest. A list of inflated agency rates struck through to a lower price reads as a pitch. Sophisticated buyers spot the second one right away and trust everything else on the page less. If you itemise, itemise real numbers.

**Setup fee: removed, and stays removed.**
No setup line, no amortised setup line hidden inside the infrastructure list.
Why: an up front fee is the single biggest thing prospects negotiate over, and every minute spent negotiating it is a minute not spent on whether the engine works. Absorbing it removes the argument entirely.

## The return on investment slide, the most rewritten part of the deck

This one section went through more versions than any other. The order it moved in:

1. Three scenario columns, cautious, expected, and strong close rates side by side.
2. A two box layout showing per month and per year.
3. One box: a numbered step by step ledger that ends in a net figure.

**Three columns became one.**
Why: three columns make the prospect pick which one they believe, and they always pick the cautious one. Worse, comparing columns costs them attention they should be spending on the mechanism. One honest, middle of the road scenario walked through slowly beats three they have to referee.

**The two box month and year split was rejected as cluttered.**
Why: the same number twice, in two frames, reads as padding. Show the monthly number and let the prospect multiply.

**The final layout puts the multiple on the left, the money on the right.**
The return multiple sits in its own bordered box. The net figure is right aligned beside it. That went through three versions: plain big text, then boxed, then the split.
Why: the multiple is the headline they repeat to a business partner later. The money is the number they feel. Giving each its own space stops one swallowing the other.

**The ledger is written in plain sentences.**
Rows read like "we send this many emails", "that books you this many calls", "you close this share of them", "minus what we charge", "net to you". Not a formula.
Why: a prospect who cannot follow the arithmetic top to bottom does not believe the result, no matter how good it looks.

**Every number in the deck gets recomputed when the price changes.**
When pricing moved to two tiers, the scenario cards were still doing the old arithmetic, and one summary card was still quoting a stale multiple. It took a dedicated pass to catch.
Rule that came out of it: when you change one number, search the whole file for every other number derived from it. There are always more references than you remember.

**Summary slides use one specific number, never a range.**
Before: "between eleven and thirty three new clients".
Now: the single expected case.
Why: a range on a summary card reads as a hedge. Pick the middle and own it. Ranges belong in the small print, if anywhere.

## Cost of inaction

**Generic anchors were replaced with the prospect's own numbers.**
Before: a placeholder deal value used for every prospect.
Now: their stated deal value, their stated close rate, multiplied out to a monthly, yearly, and three year figure.
Why: a generic number is a number they argue with. Their own number, repeated back, is a number they already agreed to.

**Say out loud that the figure is conservative.**
The slide states it counts first year value only and ignores renewals, so the true cost is higher.
Why: a prospect's instinct on any big loss figure is to discount it. Pre discounting it yourself removes that reflex and buys credibility for every other number in the deck.

**Do not reference a number before you have shown it.**
The cost of inaction slide sits well before pricing, so it cannot compare itself to the fee.
Why: forward references make the prospect flip back and lose the thread.

## Proof and case studies

**Video case studies are thumbnail links, not embedded players.**
Why: embedded players fail intermittently inside a single file page, and a broken player on a proof slide is worse than no proof slide. A thumbnail image with a play button that opens the video in a new tab never fails.

**Thumbnails are stored in the deck folder, not hotlinked.**
Why: hotlinked images depend on someone else's service being up and reachable from wherever the prospect opens the file. A local copy always renders.

**Thumbnails are locked to a 16 by 9 frame.**
Why: without a fixed aspect ratio the three cards ended up different heights and the row looked broken.

**The headline stat on a case study changed from revenue to meetings booked.**
Before: a monthly revenue figure for one client.
Now: the number of qualified calls per month.
Why: the deck sells meetings. Proof should be denominated in the same unit as the promise. A revenue figure also invites the question of how much of it the engine can claim.

**Claim wording is exact and does not drift.**
One case study says a client was acquired two years after onboarding. It is never "three years". Another deck had proof attributed to the wrong company entirely, and that took a dedicated fix commit.
Why: the one thing a prospect can verify is the claim. Get it wrong once and the rest of the deck is downgraded to marketing.

**Unverified stats get cut, not softened.**
One deck went through a correctness pass that removed every number that could not be traced to a source, and replaced them with a slide showing the prospect's goal and the gap to it.
Why: a number you cannot defend on the call is a liability, not an asset.

**When you pitch an existing client, the proof must be theirs, not yours.**
Why: someone who has already worked with you does not need convincing that you can do the work. They need to see what happened in their own account.

**Never put a link on a client facing button without opening it first.**
Two example links in one build were transcribed wrong from a call recording, and a third pointed at a page that had been renamed. Every outbound link is checked before the deck ships.

## Layout and spacing fixes that kept recurring

These came back so many times across so many decks that they are now hard rules.

**Cards in a row must be the same height.** Any grid of cards stretches to equal height. Staggered card bottoms are the single most common thing that makes a deck look unfinished, and it appeared in at least four separate builds before it was written down.

**Every content box is the full width of the deck.** One recap card was built narrower than everything around it and looked wrong immediately. Nothing sits narrower than the rest of the page.

**Three line balance.** Any paragraph inside a multi column row wraps to three lines maximum, and the columns should look balanced. If one runs to four, trim filler words. Never trim facts to make it fit.

**No widow words.** Headlines and subtitles use balanced text wrapping. Callouts, timeline rows, and bottom of slide copy must fit on one line at a normal laptop width. If a sentence breaks with two words alone on a second line, rewrite the sentence.

**Descenders were getting clipped.** Gradient coloured headline text had its tails cut off on letters like g, y, and p. Fixed with extra line height and a little bottom padding, then written down so it does not come back.

**Paragraphs inside cards became bullets.** Several decks converted pillar descriptions from prose blocks to short scannable lines. A prospect skims a slide in about four seconds. Prose does not survive that.

**Buttons inside cards are pinned to the card bottom** so they line up across a row, instead of floating wherever the text above them ends.

**Tick marks in check lists broke when padding was added** to the list items, because the tick is positioned absolutely. There is now a dedicated class for that layout and a note not to hand pad it.

**Icons sit inline to the left of a big number**, not stacked above a label. Stacking made the number look like a caption instead of the point.

**Big numbers baseline align.** The currency symbol and the "per month" suffix align to the bottom of the number rather than floating at the top with margin hacks.

## Copy rules that got escalated to hard rules

Each of these started as a one off correction and became a rule after it happened more than once.

**Zero long dashes.** An audit found two hiding in the frequently asked questions section of the master template after everyone assumed the file was clean. There is now a check that counts them and expects zero.

**No full stops on main headlines.** Slide titles end without a period. It reads as a title rather than a sentence fragment.

**Never abbreviate the company name.** Initials in a client facing deck read as internal shorthand.

**Refund language stays general in the headline, specific in the small print.** The big text says "your money back". The exact mechanism lives in the fine print underneath.
Why: a precise refund promise in large type invites a lawyer sized conversation about edge cases. The mechanism still has to be there, just not competing with the headline.

**"Steady state" was banned.** It implies a ceiling. Replaced with "from month three onward".

**One currency per deck.** Mixing symbols in a single document is an instant credibility hit. The template exists in separate currency editions rather than trying to handle both.

**Do not promise volume, describe a target.** Especially to anyone who has run outbound before. They know the difference and they respect the honesty.

**The free front end offer is never the prospect's core product.** One build labelled the main product as the cold offer and it had to be corrected. The cold offer is a small, no risk deliverable that costs the recipient nothing to accept.

**Nothing goes in the deck that is not traceable.** Market size, deal value, close rate, all sourced from what the prospect actually said or from documented research, with a footnote where relevant. Context files are not a source, they go stale.

## Structural changes, and why each slide moved

**A cost of inaction slide was inserted early.** It sits after the problem and solution and before the mechanism. Inserting it forced a renumber of every slide after it, which is how everyone learned that the slide numbers are load bearing: the navigation dots are built from them, so a gap breaks the whole deck.

**A risk and compliance slide was added late, titled as the question a prospect actually asks.** It answers, in order: is this spam, is this legal, and is my domain safe. It sits directly after the guarantee and before the final comparison.
Why: this objection was coming up on nearly every call, always at the moment of decision, and always unprompted. An objection that reliable deserves a slide, not an ad lib.

**A team slide was added with real photographs.** Not letter avatars. It shows the market rate for the people involved struck through to nothing, because their time is included.
Why: photographs of actual humans changed how the engine was perceived, from software you rent to people you hire.

**The final slide order was locked after several rewrites.** It is: summary numbers, then the call to action, then proof, then honest questions, then footer links. The slide is top aligned rather than vertically centred so the button lands above the fold on a laptop.
Why: an earlier version buried the button under the proof section and prospects never scrolled to it. Numbers first because they are a reminder, button second because that is the point of the slide, proof third to catch anyone still hesitating.

**The four summary number cards were dropped in one pass and put straight back.** They look redundant next to a call to action. They are not. They are the last thing the prospect reads before deciding, and they compress the whole deck into four figures.

**A persistent call to action button was added to every slide.** Fixed in the top corner, present from slide one.
Why: prospects do not always decide at the end. Sometimes they decide on the pricing slide, and there was nothing to click.

**Engagement tracking became mandatory.** The deck reports opens, unlocks, which slides were reached, and every button click, all stitched into one session per viewer.
Why: without it, sending a deck is a black hole. With it, you can see that someone opened it, read nine slides, stopped at pricing, and came back twice. That tells you what to say on the follow up call.

## What is still unresolved

These are genuine open tensions in the sources, not tidy conclusions.

**Slide count versus attention span.** The history swings hard in both directions. Early decks ran to twenty five slides. One was cut to eleven after direct call feedback that it was too long, then cut again to ten for a per prospect version. Meanwhile the master template grew from nineteen to twenty as new objections earned their own slides. Nobody has settled whether the right answer is a long deck you skip through or a short deck you say more over. Both are in active use.

**Generic template versus per prospect customisation.** The master template has a long list of things that must never be changed, and a short list of fields that should be. But every per prospect build in the history rewrote considerably more than the short list allowed, and those builds are the ones that closed. The lock list may be describing an ideal rather than the practice.

**Two live sources disagree about the return on investment slide.** The master template still carries three close rate scenarios. The per prospect build guide says explicitly to show one scenario only and never three. Both files are current. Whichever one you copy from, you inherit its answer.

**Transparency was half retracted, not resolved.** The itemised cost list stayed. The words explaining why it is itemised were deleted. That is a workable compromise, but it leaves a slide that invites a question the deck no longer answers on the page. It now has to be answered out loud, which means it depends on the person presenting.

**The guarantee and the risk sharing argument overlap.** The deck carries a refund guarantee if a minimum number of meetings is not hit inside sixty days. The contract position elsewhere is that the pay per result structure is itself the risk share and no separate guarantee should be added. Both exist. Nobody has reconciled which one is doing the work.

**The team slide is a value stack, in a deck that bans value stacks.** Showing salary rates struck through to zero is exactly the anchoring move the pricing rules reject everywhere else. It tests well, so it stays. It is still an inconsistency.

**Password gating fights iteration.** A gated deck is encrypted at publish time, so any copy change means re encrypting and redeploying, and the published file cannot be read back as a source. More than one build wasted time cloning an encrypted page. The workaround is to always keep a readable source file next to the published one, but the friction is real and it discourages small fixes after sending.
